# The 15 Thinking Frameworks

Each entry: **what it is · the key question it forces · how to apply · common failure mode.**
Pick the ones that fit the problem (see the selection map in SKILL.md). Go deep on
a few rather than shallow on all.

---

## 1. 第一性原理 (First Principles)
- **What**: Strip an issue down to fundamental, verifiable facts, then reason up
  from there — instead of reasoning by analogy or "how it's usually done".
- **Key question**: What do we *know is true* at the bottom, independent of
  convention or assumption?
- **Apply**: List the base facts/constraints (physics, cost, math, definitions).
  Rebuild the solution from those. Challenge every "we have to" — is it a real
  constraint or an inherited habit?
- **Failure mode**: Mistaking a strong assumption for a fundamental fact.

## 2. 矛盾分析法 (Contradiction Analysis)
- **What**: Every situation has multiple tensions; one is usually dominant and
  drives everything else.
- **Key question**: What is the *principal* contradiction here, and what are the
  secondary ones? Which side of it is the leading aspect?
- **Apply**: Enumerate the competing forces. Rank them. Focus effort on the
  principal contradiction — solving it often dissolves the secondary ones.
- **Failure mode**: Spreading attention evenly across all tensions.

## 3. MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive)
- **What**: Break a problem into buckets that don't overlap and together cover
  everything.
- **Key question**: Are my categories non-overlapping? Do they leave any gap?
- **Apply**: Choose one clear dividing principle per level. Test for overlaps and
  for missing cases. Use it to structure options, causes, or segments.
- **Failure mode**: Categories that secretly overlap or miss an edge case.

## 4. 5Why (Five Whys)
- **What**: Ask "why" repeatedly to move from symptom to root cause.
- **Key question**: Why did *that* happen? …and why did *that*?
- **Apply**: Start at the observed problem, ask why ~5 times until you hit a cause
  that is actionable and, if fixed, prevents recurrence. Beware branching — a
  problem may have several root causes.
- **Failure mode**: Stopping at a proximate cause, or forcing a single linear chain.

## 5. 正反合 (Thesis–Antithesis–Synthesis)
- **What**: Argue the position, then argue the strongest opposing case, then
  integrate into a higher judgment.
- **Key question**: What's the best case *for*? The best case *against*? What
  survives both?
- **Apply**: Steelman both sides (not strawman the opposition). The synthesis
  should resolve or transcend the conflict, not just split the difference.
- **Failure mode**: A weak antithesis, or a "synthesis" that's just a lazy average.

## 6. 六顶思考帽 (Six Thinking Hats)
- **What**: Examine an issue from six separate angles so no dimension is skipped.
- **Key question**: What do the facts / feelings / risks / benefits / creative
  options / process each say?
- **Apply**: White = data & facts; Red = intuition & emotion; Black = risks &
  caution; Yellow = benefits & value; Green = new ideas; Blue = process &
  next-step control. Run each briefly and deliberately.
- **Failure mode**: Letting one hat (usually Black or Yellow) dominate.

## 7. 多元思维模型 (Latticework of Mental Models)
- **What**: Analyze through several disciplines, because single-discipline framing
  distorts (the "man with a hammer").
- **Key question**: What do economics / psychology / engineering / game theory /
  organizational behavior each reveal about this?
- **Apply**: Economics = incentives, opportunity cost, supply/demand. Psychology =
  motivation, bias. Engineering = margins of safety, bottlenecks, feedback loops.
  Game theory = strategic interaction, equilibria. Organization = coordination,
  agency. Pick the 2–3 lenses that bite hardest.
- **Failure mode**: Name-dropping disciplines without extracting a concrete insight.

## 8. 二八法则 (Pareto Principle)
- **What**: A small share of inputs drives most of the output.
- **Key question**: Which ~20% of causes/actions/factors produce ~80% of the result?
- **Apply**: Identify the vital few vs. the trivial many. Concentrate resources on
  the vital few; consciously de-prioritize the rest.
- **Failure mode**: Treating 80/20 as literal math instead of a heuristic to find
  leverage.

## 9. 艾森豪威尔矩阵 (Eisenhower Matrix)
- **What**: Prioritize actions by urgency × importance.
- **Key question**: Is this important? Is it urgent?
- **Apply**: Important+Urgent → do now. Important+Not urgent → schedule (this is the
  high-leverage quadrant). Urgent+Not important → delegate/minimize. Neither →
  drop. Watch for urgency crowding out importance.
- **Failure mode**: Living in the urgent column and starving important-not-urgent work.

## 10. 可证伪原则 (Falsifiability)
- **What**: A claim is only meaningful if there's evidence that could prove it wrong.
- **Key question**: What observation would show this conclusion is false? Can we
  cheaply run that test?
- **Apply**: Turn conclusions into testable hypotheses with explicit failure
  conditions. Prefer beliefs you've tried to disprove.
- **Failure mode**: Unfalsifiable claims dressed as insight; confirmation-seeking.

## 11. 反身性理论 (Reflexivity)
- **What**: Perception shapes reality and reality reshapes perception — a two-way
  feedback loop (Soros). Especially strong in markets, reputation, hype, morale.
- **Key question**: How do participants' beliefs change the very thing they're
  observing, and how does that feed back?
- **Apply**: Map the loop: belief → action → changed fundamentals → changed belief.
  Look for self-reinforcing bubbles/crashes and where the loop breaks.
- **Failure mode**: Treating fundamentals as fixed and independent of perception.

## 12. 人类误判心理学 (Psychology of Human Misjudgment)
- **What**: Munger's catalog of cognitive biases that systematically distort judgment.
- **Key question**: Which biases might be steering *my own* reasoning here?
- **Apply**: Check high-frequency ones: confirmation bias, incentive-caused bias,
  social proof, authority, availability, sunk cost, anchoring, loss aversion,
  overconfidence, commitment/consistency. Name the specific ones at play, not the
  whole list.
- **Failure mode**: Spotting others' biases while ignoring your own.

## 13. 第二层思维 (Second-Level Thinking)
- **What**: Howard Marks — go beyond the obvious first reaction to what the
  consensus already prices in, and where it's wrong.
- **Key question**: What does everyone already believe, and where is that consensus
  blind or mispriced? What do I know that they don't, or interpret differently?
- **Apply**: State the first-level (consensus) view, then find the non-obvious
  second-level view. Edge comes only from being both different *and* right.
- **Failure mode**: Being contrarian for its own sake, or just restating consensus.

## 14. 黑天鹅思想 (Black Swan / Antifragility)
- **What**: Taleb — rare, high-impact, hard-to-predict events dominate outcomes;
  design to survive and benefit from disorder.
- **Key question**: What low-probability, high-impact events could blow this up? How
  do we become antifragile rather than merely robust?
- **Apply**: Identify tail risks and hidden fragilities (leverage, single points of
  failure, negative asymmetry). Prefer options with capped downside and open-ended
  upside (convexity). Build slack, optionality, redundancy.
- **Failure mode**: Planning only for the expected case; over-fitting to past data.

## 15. 哥德尔不完备定理 (Gödel Incompleteness — as metacognitive lens)
- **What**: Any sufficiently rich formal system contains true statements it cannot
  prove from within. Used here as a metaphor: every analytical frame has blind
  spots it cannot see from inside itself.
- **Key question**: What can this framing *not* decide? What did I have to assume,
  and what lies outside the boundary I drew?
- **Apply**: End the analysis by naming its own limits — unexamined assumptions,
  out-of-scope factors, and questions that need a different frame or fresh data.
- **Failure mode**: Presenting the analysis as complete and self-certifying.
